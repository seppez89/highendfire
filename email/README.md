The complete newsletter system: concept, calendar, copy, automation, metrics.
Built 2026-08-06 against live `index.html` inventory (38 listings, $76,700 retail).

## 1. The concept and the name

### RAKING LIGHT

*One card. One angle. Every Thursday.*

**Raking light** is the technique a conservator uses on a painting: put a lamp almost
flat against the surface and every brushstroke, every crack, every repair jumps out —
things you cannot see under ordinary light.

Every collector already does this. You tilt a card under a lamp to read the foil, to
find the whitening on an edge, to see what the scan never shows you. Same move, same
instinct — the art world just gave it a name first.

That is the whole brand in two words: **cards examined the way paintings are examined.**
It says "we take this seriously as art" without ever having to claim it, and it earns
the slow-macro, raking-light-on-foil filming style you already shoot in.

**The format, fixed every week (this is what makes it automatable):**

1. **THE ANGLE** — 40–70 words. One observation, one discovery, one thing you noticed.
   No greeting, no "hope you're well", no "this week in Pokémon".
2. **THE LOOK** — 150–250 words. The actual story or lesson. One idea only.
3. **IN THE CASE** — the product connection. One card. Sometimes none.
4. **ONE THING** — the primary CTA. One link, one action.
5. **THE FOOTNOTE** — the soft CTA. Reply, want list, watchlist. Never a buy button.

A fixed masthead date and a fixed sign-off — *"See you at the same angle
next Thursday."* — do the ritual work. Collectors respond to serial things. Give them
a series to collect.

**Sender name:** `Jonathon at High End Fire` (a person, not a shop).
**Send:** Thursday, 6:30pm ACST. Evening browsing, payday-adjacent, ahead of weekend hunting.

## 2. Why they keep opening it

Six reasons, in order of how much work each one does:

1. **It is one thing, not a digest.** Every issue is a single card or a single idea,
   readable in 90 seconds on a phone in a queue. A newsletter that costs nothing to
   read gets opened out of habit rather than intention — and habit is the whole game
   on a small list.
2. **The payoff is knowledge they can use tonight.** After a Raking Light issue a
   reader can pick up a card they already own and see something new in it. That is a
   free, immediate, repeatable reward. Price newsletters can't do this — the reader
   gets a number they can't act on.
3. **It flatters the reader's identity.** Not "here's what's new in stock" but "here's
   something most people looking at this card will never notice." The implicit message
   is *you are the kind of person who notices*. Collectors buy identity long before
   they buy cardboard.
4. **It never asks for much.** One product, one link, one soft ask. Nothing to brace
   against, so nothing to unsubscribe from. The single fastest way to kill a small
   list is to make every open feel like a sales call.
5. **Serial curiosity.** Issues reference each other ("remember the cutaway house card"),
   and some close a loop the next week opens. Readers stay for the series.
6. **A human replies.** When someone answers an issue, you answer back — personally,
   same day. On a list this size that is not a nicety, it is the actual growth engine:
   replies teach the inbox providers you are wanted mail, and they convert.

**What we deliberately do not do:** price predictions, "investment" framing, restock
blasts, countdown timers, "only 2 left!", or anything that would be identical if a
different shop sent it.

**On market talk (revised 2026-08-06):** the letter *does* comment on where the market
is — readers ask, and dodging it makes you look evasive. The rule is that market talk is
**observation, never forecast**, and it must earn its place by leading somewhere useful.
Say what you're seeing in your own sold prices and at your own table. Never quote a
percentage you can't source from your own books. Never say what a card will do next.
The market paragraph exists to set up the cards, not to be a market report.

## 3. The balance: education / entertainment / community / selling

Per issue, by word count:

| Element | Share | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Education | ~45% | The technique, the era, the detail, the grading reality |
| Entertainment / story | ~30% | The narrative wrapper — the artist, the discovery, the case |
| Community | ~15% | The reply prompt, reader answers quoted, want lists |
| Selling | ~10% | One product, named plainly, with a price and a link |

Across 12 issues, by issue type:

- **8 issues** — pure story, product mentioned as a doorway, not an offer.
- **2 issues** — ranked list / liquidity (Weeks 6 and 11). These carry the most
  commercial weight and they are also your proven social format.
- **1 issue** — no product at all (Week 9). This is not wasted; it is the issue that
  buys you the right to sell in the other eleven.
- **1 issue** — community/planning (Week 12), with a subscriber-only offer attached.

**The rule that keeps this honest:** the product is the *evidence for* the story, never
the *reason for* it. If you cannot write the issue without the product, the issue is an
ad. Bin it and write another.

### The one strategic call you should make now

Your inventory data says something the email plan has to respect. 73% of your capital
sits in four items over $5,000; you have exactly one listing under $50; and no completed
online card orders to date. So:

- **Under $600 → sell it in the email.** Direct link, price, buy button. This band is
  where a warm list can actually transact, and where the newsletter should do its work.
- **Over $1,000 → never put a buy button on it.** Nobody adds a $14,000 Gold Star Pikachu
  to a cart from a newsletter. Those pieces get *"reply and I'll send you the raking-light
  video and the sold comps"* — a conversation, not a checkout. The high-end pieces earn
  their keep in this newsletter as **proof of standard**, not as line items. Their job is
  to make the $140 Mewtwo feel like it came from a serious case.

That split alone is the difference between a newsletter that converts and one that
photocopies your shop page.

## 4. Subscriber segments

Six tags. Keep it to six — more than that and you will stop using them.

| Tag | Who | What changes for them |
|---|---|---|
| `art` | Artwork / illustration collectors | Default. Gets every issue as written. |
| `graded` | Slab buyers | Extra line on grade, cert, population where you have it |
| `sealed` | Sealed / booster box collectors | Gets sealed-specific P.S. and first call on box restocks |
| `highend` | $1,000+ buyers | Gets the "reply for a viewing" CTA instead of a buy link |
| `starter` | Budget-conscious, under ~$200 | Product connection swaps to the sub-$200 equivalent |
| `tcg-other` | Dragon Ball / One Piece collectors | Gets the DBS and OP issues; excluded from Pokémon-only offers |

Also useful, not a "segment" so much as a flag: `sa-local` for South Australian
subscribers who'd come to a show table. One tick box on the preference email.

**How the tags get set:** click-tagging in Welcome Email 3 (see `welcome/02-preferences.md`).
The reader clicks the collections they care about; the click applies the tag. No forms,
no fields, no friction. Anyone who doesn't click stays `art` — which is the right default,
because that is the brand.

**Rule:** never send fewer than the whole list an issue. Segments change *one block* of
an email (the product connection and the CTA), never whether the email arrives. On a list
this small, splitting sends destroys your data before it tells you anything.

## 5. The automation workflow

```
NEW SUBSCRIBER (watchlist form / exit popup / footer / sell page / show QR code)
        │
        ├─ instantly ──▶  WELCOME 0 — "You're in. Here's the first thing to look at."
        │                  (delivers the promised thing immediately)
        │
        ├─ +2 days ────▶  WELCOME 1 — the brand story. Why this shop exists.
        │
        ├─ +4 days ────▶  WELCOME 2 — preferences. Click-tagging. Sets segments.
        │
        ├─ +7 days ────▶  WELCOME 3 — the first-purchase email. One card, one offer,
        │                  free tracked post on the first order.
        │
        └─ +10 days ───▶  JOIN THE WEEKLY LIST
                          ↓
                   RAKING LIGHT — every Thursday 6:30pm ACST, 12 issues → loop
                          ↓
                   TRIGGERED (event-based, not scheduled):
                     • Watchlist match → "the one you asked about just landed"
                     • Back-in-stock → only to people who asked for that item
                     • Abandoned checkout → +4h, one email, no discount, no timer
                     • Post-purchase +3 days → "how did it arrive?" (photo request)
                     • Post-purchase +30 days → "one card that pairs with it"
                     • Lapsed 90 days no open → "still want these?" then suppress
```

**A subscriber who joins mid-sequence never gets a Thursday issue until day 10.** That
gap is deliberate. The welcome sequence is where trust gets built; dropping a stranger
straight into a mid-series issue wastes the highest-attention window you will ever have with them.

## 6. Ethical conversion methods (ranked by what will work for you)

1. **The want list — your single highest-value mechanism.** Not "join our watchlist",
   but *"tell me the exact card you're hunting and I'll message you if one crosses my
   table."* Every issue's footnote drives it. It turns a newsletter into a buying
   service, it makes your sourcing demand-led instead of guess-led, and it directly
   fixes the concentration problem: you stop buying what you like and start buying
   what a named person already asked for. Keep it as a simple reply-to-email list —
   a spreadsheet of `name / card / max budget / date asked` beats any software here.
2. **Back-in-stock and match alerts.** Only ever to the person who asked for that item.
   One email, no urgency language: "You asked about a Fossil Dragonite. One came in.
   Here it is. If it's gone by the time you look, I'll keep watching."
3. **Product spotlight inside the story.** The card is the example in the essay. This is
   the default mechanism in 8 of 12 issues, and it converts because the reader has just
   spent 90 seconds learning to appreciate the exact thing being sold.
4. **Replies as a sales channel.** Every high-end piece gets "reply and I'll send you
   the raking-light video and the sold comps." That video is a two-minute phone clip.
   It is also the single most persuasive asset you own for a $4,000 card, and it costs
   you nothing to make.
5. **Subscriber-only, twice a quarter, and make it *access*, not discount.** First look
   at new stock 24 hours before it hits the site, or free tracked post AU-wide, or
   free grading submission handling. Discounting a $600 card trains the list to wait
   for discounts. Giving them the first look trains them to open the email.
6. **Wish lists at the show.** The August table gets a card that says *"can't find it
   here? Tell me what you're hunting"* with a QR to a one-field form. Walk-up demand,
   captured, into the same welcome sequence.

**Never:** fake scarcity, invented countdowns, "prices are going up", "this will be a
grail in five years", made-up testimonials, or anything implying a card is a guaranteed
investment. Say what is observable — supply, the raw-versus-graded spread, what sold
comps show — and let the reader draw the conclusion.

## 7. Turning Instagram/TikTok carousels into issues (without copy-pasting)

Your carousel system (`hef-cameo-carousel/`) already produces the raw material. The
translation is not a format change, it is a **change of what the piece is for**.

A carousel has to work for a stranger in 1.2 seconds. A newsletter is read by someone
who already let you into their inbox. So:

| The carousel | The issue |
|---|---|
| 7 cards, one line each | **1 card** from those 7, 250 words deep |
| The claim ("this one sells instantly") | The **reason** behind the claim |
| Ends on an engagement question | Ends on one link and one reply prompt |
| Broad, for reach | Specific, for the person who already cares |

**The repurposing move, in five steps:**

1. **Wait for the data.** Only convert carousels that actually landed. Your "6 most
   liquid cards" post is the proof case — that one earned an issue.
2. **Pick the single slide people stopped on.** Instagram/TikTok will tell you where
   the swipe-throughs died or where the comments clustered. That slide is your issue.
3. **Ask the question the slide couldn't answer.** The slide said Card X sells instantly.
   The issue answers *why* — who wants it, what they're comparing it to, what the
   raw-versus-slab spread looks like, why the art makes it move.
4. **Add the thing you'd never put on social:** your own opinion, a mistake you made,
   what you paid attention to at a show, the one you passed on and regret. This is
   what makes the email worth an inbox rather than a scroll.
5. **Close a different loop.** Carousel ends with "which one's your favourite?"; issue
   ends with "reply and tell me what you're hunting" — and you actually reply.

**Run it in reverse too, and this is the better direction:** write the issue first
on Thursday, then next week cut the sharpest 7 lines out of it into the carousel
template. You get a stronger post (it has an argument underneath it) and the newsletter
becomes the top of your content pipeline instead of the leftovers.

## 8. Metrics for a small list

Open rate is close to useless at your size — Apple Mail Privacy Protection inflates it,
and 40% of 90 people is 36 people, which is noise. Track these instead, weekly, in one
spreadsheet. Absolute numbers, not just percentages, until you're past ~500 subscribers.

| Metric | Where from | What good looks like on a small list |
|---|---|---|
| **Subscribers added** | ESP | The only growth number that matters. Track by source. |
| **Unique clicks** (count, not %) | ESP | The real attention metric. Aim 8–15% of delivered. |
| **Click-to-open** | ESP | 15%+ means the story earned the click. Below 8% = weak CTA. |
| **Replies** | Inbox | **The headline metric.** 3–5 replies per issue at 100 subs is excellent. |
| **Want-list entries** | Your spreadsheet | Target 1–3 per issue. Compounding asset. |
| **Product page views from email** | Vercel Analytics + UTM | Did the story move them to the card? |
| **Watchlist sign-ups from email** | Form notifications tagged by source | Mid-funnel intent. |
| **Checkout starts** | Stripe dashboard (sessions created) | The step before revenue. Watch the gap. |
| **Purchases + revenue** | Stripe | Attribute by UTM and by asking "how'd you find it?" |
| **Unsubscribes + spam** | ESP | Under 0.5% and 0.1%. A spike = you got salesy. |
| **List → revenue per subscriber** | Revenue ÷ subs | Compute monthly. This is your list's actual worth. |

**Tag every link.** `?utm_source=newsletter&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=rl-001` — without
this, Vercel Analytics cannot tell you a thing about whether the email worked.

**Weekly review, 10 minutes, four questions:** How many replies? Which link got clicked?
Did anyone check out? What was the one thing I'd change about that issue? Write the
answers down. Twelve of those in a row will teach you more than any benchmark table.

## 9. Experimentation plan

One variable at a time. On a list under ~500 you cannot run statistically valid A/B
splits — the sample is too small — so run **sequential tests**: change one thing for
three issues in a row, compare against the previous three, keep whatever wins.

**Weeks 1–3 — subject lines.** Baseline: curiosity-gap, lowercase-ish, under 45 chars.
Then test three of these angles, one per issue, and log unique clicks (not opens):
- Curiosity gap — *"Two cards I'd hang on a wall"*
- Specific detail — *"There's a Poké Ball hidden on this card"*
- Direct callout — *"For anyone who's ever tilted a card under a lamp"*
- Number/list — *"Six cards that sell the day they arrive"*
- Flat and factual — *"Issue 004: raw vs graded, honestly"*

**Weeks 4–6 — length.** Issues 4–6 run at ~180 words (a genuinely short read). Compare
click rate and reply rate against issues 1–3 at ~350 words. Most collector lists reward
short. Yours might not — the art angle rewards depth. Find out rather than assume.

**Weeks 7–9 — CTA.** Same issue structure, three CTA styles:
- Week 7: a plain text link on its own line
- Week 8: a single orange button
- Week 9: a link *inside* the last sentence of the story, no separate CTA block
Track click-to-open for each. Text-link-in-story usually wins on collector lists because
it doesn't look like advertising, but a button wins when the offer is concrete.

**Weeks 10–12 — send time.** Hold everything else constant:
- Week 10: Thursday 6:30pm ACST (control)
- Week 11: Sunday 9:00am ACST
- Week 12: Tuesday 12:30pm ACST
Compare click count in the first 6 hours. Whichever window gets the fastest clicks wins;
early clicks also help your sender reputation.

**Always-on, no test needed:** send from a person's name, keep one CTA, and reply to
every reply within 24 hours. Those three are not worth testing — they are the baseline.

**Log it.** One row per issue: `issue / subject / variable tested / delivered / unique
clicks / CTO / replies / want-list adds / checkout starts / revenue`. The log is the
experiment. Without it you are just sending emails.
